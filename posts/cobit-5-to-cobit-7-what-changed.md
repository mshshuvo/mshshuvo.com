I passed the COBIT 5 Foundation exam in September 2016. At the time, COBIT 5 was *the* way to talk about IT governance: five principles, seven enablers, 37 processes and a goals cascade that tied every IT activity back to business goals.

A decade later, ISACA has released **COBIT 7** (launched 27 October 2026), and COBIT 2019, the version most organisations moved to, will be retired on **31 December 2027**. COBIT 5 itself is already officially retired. Yet I still see COBIT 5 process IDs quoted in policies, audit programmes and job descriptions.

So this is the comparison I wanted for myself: what changed across the three versions, what matters in practice, and what to do if your organisation is still running on COBIT 5.

## The short version

| | **COBIT 5** (2012) | **COBIT 2019** | **COBIT 7** (2026) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Status** | Retired | Sunsets 31 Dec 2027 | Current |
| **Principles** | 5 principles | 6 governance-system principles plus 3 framework principles | No change announced |
| **Building blocks** | 7 enablers | 7 components | Organisational structures described by role, not task |
| **Processes / objectives** | 37 processes | 40 governance and management objectives | Objectives updated with expanded activities |
| **Tailoring** | Goals cascade | 11 design factors, focus areas, 4-step design guide | 2 new design factors, design guide cut to 3 steps |
| **Assessment** | ISO/IEC 15504 process capability | CMMI-based capability levels 0–5 | New performance management and assessment module (board, high-level and detailed) |
| **AI and emerging tech** | Not covered | Limited | AI risk scenarios, AI and cloud implementation methods |
| **Delivery** | PDF books | PDF books plus an online toolkit | Digital platform; basic access is free |

## COBIT 5 to COBIT 2019: the big structural shift

Most of the conceptual change happened in 2019, not 2026. If you learned COBIT 5, these are the things that moved.

**Enablers became components.** The seven building blocks stayed recognisable (processes; organisational structures; principles, policies and procedures; information; culture, ethics and behaviour; people, skills and competencies; services, infrastructure and applications), but COBIT 2019 treats them as components of a *governance system* rather than enablers.

**Processes became objectives.** COBIT 5's 37 processes became 40 governance and management objectives. Three were added:

- **APO14 Managed Data**: data finally became a first-class management objective.
- **BAI11 Managed Projects**: projects separated from programmes.
- **MEA04 Managed Assurance**: assurance became something you manage, not just something auditors do to you.

**One size stopped fitting all.** COBIT 5's goals cascade was elegant on paper but hard to apply. COBIT 2019 introduced **11 design factors** (enterprise strategy, enterprise goals, risk profile, I&T-related issues, threat landscape, compliance requirements, role of IT, sourcing model, IT implementation methods, technology adoption strategy and enterprise size) to tailor which objectives matter most for a given organisation. It also introduced **focus areas** for topics such as small and medium enterprises, cybersecurity and DevOps.

**The capability scale changed.** COBIT 5 used a process assessment model based on ISO/IEC 15504. COBIT 2019 moved to a **CMMI-based** capability scale from 0 to 5 for processes, with maturity levels for focus areas. A COBIT 5 "level 3" and a COBIT 2019 "level 3" are not the same claim.

## COBIT 2019 to COBIT 7: refinement, measurement and AI

COBIT 7 is an evolution of COBIT 2019, not a rewrite. ISACA describes it as a refresh that makes the framework "more comprehensive, accessible and practical". The concrete changes announced so far:

**Measurement gets its own module.** A new **performance management and assessment** module supports three levels of assessment: **board-level, high-level and detailed**. It comes with practitioner guidance, downloadable assessment tools and how-to videos. For auditors, this is the headline change: there is now an official, structured way to baseline current performance and track improvement.

**Design is simpler and more honest.**

- The design guide goes from **four steps to three**.
- Scoring now distinguishes whether each design factor is **forward-looking or describes the current state**.
- Two **new design factors** are added: **Organisational Health Context** and **Information/Data Management Strategies**.

**Organisational structures are described by role, not task.** This should make RACI-style mapping to real job titles much easier.

**Objectives get expanded activities.** The process components of the objectives include expanded activities. ISACA sells these separately as an *Objectives Workbook* (PDF).

**AI is finally in scope.**

- New **AI risk scenarios** have been added to the I&T risk profile design factor.
- **IT implementation methods** now account for AI and cloud.
- **I&T-related issue examples** are refreshed for AI and cybersecurity.

**It's a platform now, not a book.** COBIT 7 lives on a digital platform:

- **Basic access is free:** introduction and methodology, governance and management objectives, and the implementation guide.
- **Premium access** (US$300 for ISACA members, US$550 for non-members, one year) adds the updated design tool and three downloadable assessments.
- The design tool automates the workflow and suggests a target achievement level for each objective from your design inputs.

## What this means if you're an IT auditor

1. **Re-baseline before you compare.** Don't compare a COBIT 5 capability rating from an old report with a COBIT 2019 or COBIT 7 rating. The scales are different. Note the version and the assessment method in every finding.
2. **Update your audit programmes' references.** COBIT 5 process IDs mostly map to COBIT 2019 and 7 objectives, but not one-to-one. APO14, BAI11 and MEA04 have no COBIT 5 equivalent.
3. **Use the three assessment levels deliberately.** A board-level assessment for the audit committee and a detailed assessment for the IT function answer different questions. Pick the level that matches who will act on the finding.
4. **Add AI to your risk scoping now.** COBIT 7's AI risk scenarios give you an authoritative reference for asking management about AI governance, even before your organisation has an AI policy.

## If your organisation is still on COBIT 5: a practical path

1. **Inventory where COBIT 5 is referenced:** policies, the control framework, audit programmes, board reporting and job descriptions.
2. **Go straight to COBIT 7.** With COBIT 2019 sunsetting at the end of 2027, adopting it now means migrating twice.
3. **Start with the free basic access.** Read the methodology and the objectives before buying anything.
4. **Run the design factors honestly.** The point of tailoring is to *drop* objectives that don't matter for you, not to tick all 40.
5. **Re-map your controls once.** If you also report against ISO/IEC 27001 or NIST CSF 2.0, map them together so each control serves every framework. My free [Control Crosswalk](https://mshshuvo.com/toolkit/control-crosswalk) can help with the ISO and NIST side.
6. **Baseline with the new assessment module**, then report progress against that baseline instead of against an old COBIT 5 capability score.

## Is a COBIT 5 certificate still worth anything?

The framework has moved on, so the certificate on its own is a historical credential, and I describe mine that way: COBIT 5 Foundation, APMG International, 2016. But the thinking it taught still holds: governance is separate from management, IT exists to deliver stakeholder value, and you can't improve what you don't measure. COBIT 7 builds on the same ideas.
